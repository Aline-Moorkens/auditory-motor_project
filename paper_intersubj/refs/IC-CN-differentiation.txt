Researchers separate the cochlear nucleus (CN) and inferior colliculus (IC) contributions to the frequency-following response (FFR) by manipulating electrode geometry, stimulus frequencies, signal processing, and multichannel source modeling. Because a single scalp electrode records a composite blend of all active generators, isolating each structure requires exploiting their distinct anatomical orientations, latencies, and phase-locking limits.

1. Electrode Montages (Spatial Orientation)

Different brainstem nuclei generate electrical dipoles pointing in distinct anatomical directions, allowing researchers to favor one structure over another by changing electrode placement.
• Horizontal Montage (Earlobe-to-Earlobe or Mastoid-to-Mastoid): Placing electrodes horizontally across the head aligns with the lateral-to-medial orientation of the auditory nerve and cochlear nucleus. This setup emphasizes early, shorter-latency components (~1.2–2 ms) from the lower brainstem.
• Vertical Montage (Vertex Cz-to-Earlobe or Neck): Placing an active electrode at the top of the head (Cz) relative to an earlobe or mastoid aligns with the vertical dipole of the midbrain. This configuration maximizes signals from the inferior colliculus (~5–6 ms latency) and diminishes early caudal contributions.
• Multi-Channel Recordings: Simultaneous recording from both horizontal and vertical channels allows researchers to compare caudal and rostral activity within the exact same trial.

2. Stimulus Frequency and Modulation Rate

Because neurons lose the ability to phase-lock to rapid fluctuations as signals travel higher up the auditory pathway, stimulus frequency acts as a natural filter for isolating generators.
• High Carrier Frequencies (> 300–500 Hz): Neurons in the inferior colliculus drop off sharply in their ability to phase-lock above ~250–300 Hz. Presenting stimuli with higher frequencies isolates the cochlear nucleus and auditory nerve, which maintain temporal fidelity up to 1,000+ Hz.
• Low Frequencies & Envelopes (< 200 Hz): Slow amplitude modulations or low fundamental frequencies (F₀) strongly drive the inferior colliculus (as well as thalamic and cortical generators below ~100 Hz).

3. Group Delay and Latency Analysis

When pure tones or harmonic complexes are presented across several neighboring frequencies, researchers can calculate the phase slope (group delay) to determine the physiological travel time of the response.
• Short Group Delay (~1.5–3 ms): Indicates dominance by peripheral structures and the cochlear nucleus.
• Long Group Delay (~5–8 ms): Confirms that the recorded signal is dominated by the inferior colliculus.
• Cross-Correlation: Comparing the time lag between the acoustic stimulus waveform and the recorded EEG trace provides a direct estimate of which brainstem station is generating the primary peaks.

4. High-Density EEG and MEG Source Localization

Modern neuroimaging moves beyond single-channel electrodes to spatially reconstruct where signals originate inside the brain.
• High-Density EEG (64+ channels): Using multichannel EEG source imaging alongside dipole modeling or Independent Component Analysis (ICA), researchers can mathematically unmix overlapping electrical fields into distinct subcortical (CN/IC) and cortical components.
• Magnetoencephalography (MEG): Unlike EEG, magnetic fields pass through the skull without spatial smearing. Distributed source modeling with MEG-FFR recordings separates deep brainstem activity (CN and IC) from primary auditory cortex contributions. However, MEG is naturally less sensitive to deep radial sources than EEG, making it heavily weighted toward cortical and upper midbrain activity unless paired with specialized beamforming algorithms.

5. Stimulus Polarity Subtraction vs. Addition

While primarily used to separate cochlear microphonics from neural activity, alternating stimulus polarity (condensation vs. rarefaction) also helps isolate specific neural components.
• Added Polarities (A + B): Emphasizes the envelope-following response (F₀), which is heavily shaped by nonlinear rectification and converges strongly in the inferior colliculus.
• Subtracted Polarities (A - B): Emphasizes the temporal fine structure (spectral harmonics), which relies on high-frequency phase-locking preserved primarily in the cochlear nucleus and auditory nerve.
